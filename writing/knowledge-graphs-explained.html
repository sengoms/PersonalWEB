Knowledge Graphs, Explained From Zero

A beginner's guide to connected data, with a Python example you can run

Senthilkumar Palaniappan · 30 October 2026

Imagine a friend asks, “Do you know anyone who works at a hospital and plays tennis?”

You think of Priya. She works at City Hospital, and you’ve heard her talk about tennis. You’ve connected a few things you know about her and come up with an answer.

Now imagine asking software the same question. It needs those connections written down.

That’s the starting point for a knowledge graph: record the people, places, or concepts you care about, then describe how they connect. Once those relationships are explicit, you can ask questions about them.

Start with things and relationships

Let’s give our fictional example a little more detail:

From Relationship To
You KNOWS Priya
You KNOWS Sam
Priya WORKS_AT City Hospital
Priya PLAYS Tennis
Sam PLAYS Tennis
City Hospital LOCATED_IN Atlanta

In graph terminology, the things are nodes, and the connections are edges. The label on an edge tells you what it means. WORKS_AT says something quite different from PLAYS.

The arrows matter, too. You → KNOWS → Priya records that you know Priya. It doesn’t automatically record that Priya knows you. That may feel fussy in a social example, but think of MANAGES or OWNS: reversing the arrow changes the meaning.

You can attach details as well. Priya might have a birth year; her employment relationship might have a start date. In a property graph, these details are called properties.

You’ll also encounter RDF, another way of representing graph data. It uses subject–predicate–object statements, often called triples. We’ll stick with property graphs here. If you want to explore RDF later, the W3C’s RDF primer is a useful introduction.

So what makes this a knowledge graph? Definitions vary, but usually the aim is to give the connections a consistent meaning: use stable identifiers, define the relationships, and keep enough context to interpret the facts.

Couldn’t a table do this?

Yes. A relational database can combine records about people, employers, and activities to answer our opening question efficiently.

The appeal of a graph is how directly you can express the question:

  • Find someone you know.
  • Check that they work at a hospital.
  • Check that they play tennis.

Here’s how that looks in Cypher, a graph query language:

MATCH (:Person {name: 'You'})-[:KNOWS]->(p:Person)
MATCH (p)-[:WORKS_AT]->(:Hospital)
MATCH (p)-[:PLAYS]->(:Activity {name: 'Tennis'})
RETURN DISTINCT p.name

The arrows describe the connections we want to find. Assuming the database contains the labels and relationships shown here, Priya matches.

Sam doesn’t match. We know he plays tennis, but we haven’t recorded a hospital job for him. He might have one. Our graph just doesn’t say.

Following connections is called a traversal. Each edge crossed is a hop. The route from You to Atlanta has three hops:

You → Priya → City Hospital → Atlanta

Our tennis question is a little different: it checks several branches around the same person.

Graphs become useful when your questions keep following connections. Which suppliers do two companies share? What depends on this service? Who owns the company that owns this building? A graph can make those patterns easier to write and inspect.

Whether it will be faster is a separate question. Relational databases can use indexes and efficient query plans. Graph searches can get expensive when a node has many connections or a query explores long paths. Before choosing a database, try your actual questions on data that resembles yours.

The words have to mean something

Connecting records won’t help much if you’re unclear about what they mean.

Think about healthcare. A clinician writes an abbreviation, a patient uses an everyday phrase, and a record stores an identifier. A search system needs to work out when those refer to the same thing, when one is more specific, and when they mean something different.

SNOMED CT is a clinical terminology that represents concepts and their relationships. Its hierarchy can help a search include more specific concepts beneath a broader one.

We can see the basic idea in a fictional equipment catalogue:

Specific category Relationship Broader category
Portable monitor IS_A Monitor
Bedside monitor IS_A Monitor
Monitor IS_A Equipment

Here, IS_A points from specific to broad. To find the narrower categories beneath Monitor, we follow those connections in the opposite direction.

A search for monitors can then include portable and bedside monitors, even if the records use those more specific labels.

Clinical data takes more care. An abbreviation may have several meanings, and a diagnosis code may cover more or less than a clinical concept. A mapping between systems can depend on context, edition, and rules. Drawing a connection doesn’t make the two ends equivalent.

Our equipment catalogue is an invented teaching example, rather than a clinical hierarchy. If you use actual SNOMED CT content, check its licensing arrangements.

Where AI comes in

If you’ve used AI tools, you may have come across RAG, short for retrieval-augmented generation.

The idea is to find relevant material and give it to a language model to use when writing an answer. That search might use keywords, vector similarity, database queries, or several methods together.

Vector search uses numerical representations called embeddings to find related content. It can connect passages that use different wording, but it may miss an exact dependency or fail to retrieve every member of a category.

A graph gives the system another route. In our catalogue example, it could start at Monitor, find the narrower categories, and fetch documents about those items. Keeping the paths and source records would also let someone check how the system found the material.

Approaches that bring graphs into retrieval and generation are often called GraphRAG. Microsoft’s implementation, for example, combines graph information with source text in its local search method.

Of course, the graph can be wrong. A relationship may be out of date, extracted incorrectly, or taken from a weak source. The model can also misread what it retrieves. You still need evidence for the connections and a way to check the answer against that evidence.

Build one in Python

Let’s build the people-and-hospital example using NetworkX, a Python library for working with graphs.

Install it first:

python -m pip install networkx

We’ll use MultiDiGraph, which gives us directed edges and allows more than one relationship between the same two nodes.

import networkx as nx

G = nx.MultiDiGraph()
G.add_node("You", kind="Person")
G.add_node("Priya", kind="Person")
G.add_node("Sam", kind="Person")
G.add_node("City Hospital", kind="Hospital")
G.add_node("Tennis", kind="Activity")
G.add_node("Atlanta", kind="City")

relationships = [
    ("You", "Priya", "KNOWS"),
    ("You", "Sam", "KNOWS"),
    ("Priya", "City Hospital", "WORKS_AT"),
    ("Priya", "Tennis", "PLAYS"),
    ("Sam", "Tennis", "PLAYS"),
    ("City Hospital", "Atlanta", "LOCATED_IN"),
]

for source, target, relationship in relationships:
    G.add_edge(source, target, rel=relationship)


def targets(node, relationship):
    return {
        target
        for _, target, data in G.out_edges(node, data=True)
        if data["rel"] == relationship
    }


for person in sorted(targets("You", "KNOWS")):
    hospitals = {
        employer
        for employer in targets(person, "WORKS_AT")
        if G.nodes[employer].get("kind") == "Hospital"
    }

    if hospitals and "Tennis" in targets(person, "PLAYS"):
        print(
            person,
            "works at",
            ", ".join(sorted(hospitals)),
            "and plays tennis",
        )

print(nx.shortest_path(G, "You", "Atlanta"))

Expected output:

Priya works at City Hospital and plays tennis
['You', 'Priya', 'City Hospital', 'Atlanta']

The targets function collects the things connected to a node by a particular relationship. We use it to find people you know, check their employers, and check whether they play tennis.

The final line asks a different question: what is a shortest route from You to Atlanta? That call treats every edge as one step, whatever its label. In another application, you might want to allow only certain relationship types.

Now let’s build the catalogue hierarchy. Run this after the first example:

catalogue = nx.DiGraph()
catalogue.add_edges_from([
    ("Portable monitor", "Monitor"),
    ("Bedside monitor", "Monitor"),
    ("Monitor", "Equipment"),
])

category = "Monitor"
matches = {category} | nx.ancestors(catalogue, category)

print(sorted(matches))

Expected output:

['Bedside monitor', 'Monitor', 'Portable monitor']

The function name ancestors can be confusing here. Our arrows point from a specific category towards a broader one, so the nodes that can reach Monitor are its narrower categories.

Every edge in this second graph means IS_A. The lesson is worth remembering: check what the arrow direction means in your data before choosing an algorithm.

Five habits worth keeping

  1. Start with a question. Write down what you need to answer before designing the graph. A few useful queries will give you somewhere concrete to begin.

  2. Name relationships carefully. RELATED_TO may be enough while you’re exploring. More precise labels help when you need precise answers.

  3. Give things stable identifiers. Two people can have the same name. One organisation can change its name. A name alone is rarely a dependable identifier.

  4. Keep the evidence. Record where each relationship came from and whether it has been checked. A connection proposed by a language model still needs validation.

  5. Remember that data changes. Track the edition and date of any terminology you import. SNOMED CT’s International Edition has monthly releases; other editions and products follow their own release schedules.

For a first project, go back to one question you care about. Write down the things involved, connect them, and try the query. Then check the answer.

That small exercise will tell you more about whether you need a knowledge graph than starting with a grand model of everything.
